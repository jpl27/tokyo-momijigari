The land

Before Edo: the ground the city was built on. Small numbers link to the references at the foot of the page.

The plain

The Kantō plain is the largest lowland in Japan. Mountains wall it in on the west, the north and the northeast. The bay closes it on the south. The middle of the plain is slowly sinking, which gives it this bowl shape. Most rivers of central Honshū drain into the bowl and carry mud and sand toward the bay.[7,8]

Central Tokyo sits on two kinds of ground. To the west is the Musashino upland, a raised, flat terrace. Rivers built it as a huge fan of gravel during the ice ages. To the east is the low delta, only a few metres above the sea. Later centuries called the upland Yamanote and the lowland Shitamachi. The 10-metre contour on the Five Faces map marks the edge between them.[6,7]

Volcanic ash from Fuji and Hakone covers the upland in a thick layer. Rain sinks straight through it to gravel far below. So the flat top of the terrace had little surface water. Springs came out only where valleys cut down to that water. People settled at the terrace edge and around the spring ponds, not on the dry top. The upland stayed thinly settled until Edo's aqueducts arrived in the 1600s. The first map shows the whole plain in relief, with its old and present rivers and its old provinces.[4,7,8]

The rivers

Before 1590 the Tone River did not reach the Pacific. It flowed south across the plain into Tokyo Bay. Its main channel ran roughly where the Furu-Tone and Naka rivers run today. The Watarase was a separate river then, flowing down the line of the lower Edo River. The lowland flooded often. People built where low banks of sand lifted the ground a little.[16,17]

Between 1594 and 1654, Tokugawa officials moved the Tone east. A dam at Kawamata in 1594 closed its main southward channel. From 1621, cuts at Akahori began to link the Tone to rivers flowing east. The upper Edo River was dug between 1635 and 1641. A deeper cut in 1654 finally let the Tone run to the sea at Chōshi. Scholars still argue whether this was one plan. Many date the real start to 1621 and see several aims: new rice land, boat routes and flood control.[16,17,18]

The Arakawa once joined the Tone too. In 1629 it was turned west into the Iruma River. From then on, today's Sumida carried the Arakawa past Asakusa. The straight Arakawa flood channel was dug from 1911 to 1930. The Tama River frames the terrace on the south. The Kanda's straight run through Ochanomizu is a cut made in 1620.[14,15,9]

The bay and the inlet

Tokyo Bay is shallow and sheltered. The Miura and Bōsō peninsulas pinch its mouth into a narrow channel. That keeps out most of the Pacific swell, so small boats could cross it safely. Rivers filled its head with wide, gently sloping mudflats.[7]

About 6,000 years ago the sea stood a few metres higher than it does now. It flooded the river valleys far inland, into what is now Saitama. Waves broke against the foot of the terrace from Akabane south to Ōmori. Shell mounds mark where people lived above that shore. They sit on the terrace rim, close to springs and to rich mudflats. The Ōmori mound, dug by Edward Morse in 1877, is the famous case.[1,23,26]

As the sea fell back, sand built a low spit east of today's palace. Historians call it Edo-maejima. It ran south from Kanda through Nihonbashi and Ginza toward Shinbashi. Between the spit and the castle bluff lay the shallow Hibiya inlet. The Hirakawa River emptied into its northern end, near today's Ōtemachi. Boats could land at the foot of the castle. Filling began in 1590 and had closed most of the inlet by about 1607. The second map draws this old shore over today's streets. Its lines are a sketch, not a survey.[1,12,13,9]

The region

Before Edo, power on this plain lay elsewhere. Musashi was one of the eight Kantō provinces. Its government office stood at Fuchū, on dry ground above the Tama River. The office dates from the early 700s. Ōkunitama Shrine now stands over its central buildings.[24]

Kamakura, to the south, became the seat of warrior government in 1185. Roads ran north from it across the plain like the ribs of a fan. Two of the main roads crossed the dry upland well west of Edo. Only the lower road followed the bay shore through Shinagawa, Edo and Asakusa. From about 1495 the Hōjō family ruled from Odawara, at the plain's southwest corner.[19,31]

Edo lay where the coast road met the bay and the rivers. A traveller could keep to dry sandbars there and cross the river mouths. Ships from the bay met boats coming down the inland rivers. So Edo was a crossing point, not yet a centre. The Tokugawa later made it the starting point of every main road. They set up the Tōkaidō and the Nakasendō from 1601. The third map shows these roads with the early shrines and castles.[7,19,22]
